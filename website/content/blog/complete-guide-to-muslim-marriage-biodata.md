## What a biodata is for

A marriage biodata is a short document that introduces a person and their family to another family. Its job is not to impress — it is to answer the first questions honestly, so both families can decide whether to talk.

## The sections, one by one

### 1. Basic details

Full name (or how you want it shown), date of birth, height, marital status and mother tongue. If previously married, say so clearly and mention children. Clarity here prevents awkward conversations later.

### 2. About

Three to five honest sentences: personality, interests, values and plans. Specific details ("teaches maths, enjoys cooking for the family on weekends") help far more than generic ones ("simple, caring, homely").

### 3. Education and work

Highest qualification, field, profession and where you work. Income is optional — many families share it only after the first conversation.

### 4. Religious practice

Maslak or school of thought, and an honest description of practice (prayer, fasting, Qur’an reading, Hajj or Umrah). Families value honesty here more than any particular answer.

### 5. Family

Parents’ occupations, number of brothers and sisters (and how many are married), family type (nuclear or joint) and where the family lives. Never include family members’ names or phone numbers on a document that may be forwarded.

### 6. Location

City and area, native place, and whether you are willing to relocate. NRIs should mention their country and plans.

### 7. Partner preferences

Age range, education, location, practice and marital status you are open to. Keep it reasonable; very narrow preferences hide good matches.

### 8. Photo

One clear, recent photo is enough. Decide who can see it.

## Common mistakes

- Writing age instead of date of birth (ages go out of date).
- Leaving out marital history or children.
- Copy-pasting a generic "about" paragraph.
- Putting a phone number on a biodata that gets forwarded.
- Listing ten non-negotiables.
